# Foundations: prioritization and impact

Full citations are in `biblioteca/referencias.md`.

**Lewis & Rao (2015), "The Unfavorable Economics of Measuring the Returns to Advertising", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 130(4): 1941-1973.** Across 25 field experiments, the median confidence interval on ROI was over 100 percentage points wide: even rigorous tests give wide ranges for marketing returns. *Used here:* impact is stated as a range, and single-point forecasts are refused. *Limit:* the paper studies paid display advertising; the point about noisy returns transfers, the exact width does not.

**Kohavi, Tang & Xu (2020), *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments*, Cambridge University Press.** Large effects are rare, results that look too good deserve suspicion first, and comparison groups are what separate effect from coincidence. *Used here:* the confirmation rule uses a comparison set (matched untouched pages or the same period last year), and the skill warns about before/after charts.

**Pew Research Center (22 July 2025), "Google users are less likely to click on links when an AI summary appears in the results".** Browsing data from 900 U.S. adults in March 2025 (68,879 Google searches, 12,593 with an AI summary): users clicked a traditional result link in 8% of visits with an AI summary against 15% without one, and rarely clicked the sources cited in the summary. *Used here:* a flat click forecast for informational queries must be discounted for AI summaries, and impressions and clicks are reported separately. *Limit:* U.S. adults, one month, Google only; Chilean query mix may differ.

**Google Search Central, "Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content".** Google states its ranking systems prioritize helpful, reliable information made for people. *Used here:* no plan promises a ranking from a tactic; the plan invests in pages that deserve to rank.
