# Foundations: customer economics

Full citations are in `biblioteca/referencias.md`.

**Reichheld & Sasser (1990), "Zero Defections: Quality Comes to Services", *Harvard Business Review*, September.** Argued that companies can raise profits notably by retaining just 5% more of their customers, because profit per customer grows with the age of the relationship.
*Used here:* supports retention as a CLV lever with its own return.
*Important limit:* the famous figure has been disputed (for example, an Ipsos analysis questions it because not every retained customer is profitable). That is why the skill asks to measure CLV by cohort and by channel instead of applying a general rule.

**Fader, Hardie & Lee (2005), "RFM and CLV: Using Iso-Value Curves for Customer Base Analysis", *Journal of Marketing Research* 42(4): 415-430.** Formally links RFM with customer lifetime value using a probabilistic model (Pareto/NBD for transactions and gamma-gamma for spend), validated with CDNOW customers.
*Used here:* supports that RFM contains the information needed to value customers, and that serious projections require a model, not an average.
*Limit:* this skill computes CLV as ticket × observed purchases × margin; it is a decision calculation, not a probabilistic projection. To project, the next step is BG/NBD or Pareto/NBD.

**Fader & Hardie (2009), "Probability models for customer-base analysis", *Journal of Interactive Marketing*.** Distinguishes contractual settings (you observe when the customer leaves) from non-contractual ones (the customer's "death" is not observed), which is the ecommerce case.
*Used here:* in non-contractual settings, "lost customer" is an inference; the skill treats it as a hypothesis.

**Sharp (2010), *How Brands Grow*, and Binet & Field (2013), *The Long and the Short of It*.** Growing by penetration and retaining well are not mutually exclusive: retention sustains CLV; acquisition sustains size. The optimal proportion depends on the category.
